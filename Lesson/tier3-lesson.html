00 — Orientation

Why this tier is the project

Your fusion layer is called signal-quality-adaptive fusion. Unpack that name and it makes a specific promise:

  • Signal-Quality — the system measures, live, how trustworthy each extraction method is right now.
  • Adaptive — those measurements change frame by frame as conditions change.
  • Fusion — the final answer is a blend weighted by those measurements, rather than a bet on one fixed method.

Everything hinges on the first part. If your quality metric is bad, the adaptation is arbitrary and the fusion is worse than just picking a decent method and sticking with it. If the metric is good, the whole thing works and you have a real result.

So this tier is short on new theory and long on getting one thing right.

The good news: the quality metric comes straight out of the FFT you already computed. No new transform, no extra pass over the data, no trained model. It is a ratio of two sums.
01 — The bridge

Parseval's theorem, or: energy is energy

One small result unlocks the whole tier.

Σ |x[n]|² = (1/N) Σ |X[k]|² total energy total energy in the time domain in the frequency domain

Add up the squared samples of your signal, and you get the same total as adding up the squared values of its spectrum. The Fourier transform moves energy around between frequencies but never creates or destroys any.

Why that is useful: it means you can ask "what fraction of my signal's energy sits in the heart-rate band?" and answer it entirely in the frequency domain, by adding up bins. And that fraction is exactly a measure of quality.

  • A clean pulse concentrates nearly all its energy into one narrow peak. High fraction.
  • Noise spreads energy evenly everywhere. Low fraction.

That single ratio is your metric. Everything below is refinement.

Plain English

Power here just means the square of the magnitude. Squaring makes everything positive so it can be added up, and it weights strong components more heavily than weak ones. When a paper says "power spectrum" it means the ordinary spectrum with every value squared.

02 — The metric

Spectral SNR: how much of the energy is the pulse?

Here is the metric, in full:

# 1. find the strongest peak inside the plausible band peak = argmax(power[band]) # 2. energy in a narrow window around that peak, plus its harmonic signalPower = sum(power[peak ± w]) + sum(power[2·peak ± w]) # 3. everything else in the band noisePower = sum(power[band]) − signalPower # 4. the quality score quality = signalPower / (signalPower + noisePower)

The result lands between 0 and 1. Near 1 means nearly all the energy is concentrated at one rhythm and its harmonic, which is what a real heartbeat looks like. Near 0 means the energy is spread across the band with no dominant rhythm, which is what noise looks like.

Including the harmonic in the numerator is deliberate. Tier 1 established that a real pulse necessarily produces harmonics because its waveform is sharp. Treating that harmonic as noise would penalise exactly the signals you most want to reward.

Tune w honestly, and say how you did it

The peak window w is a free parameter, and free parameters invite the question "did you tune it to make your results look good?" Tune it on one dataset (UBFC-rPPG), then leave it fixed when you evaluate on another (PURE). Report that you did. That is a cross-dataset generalisation check, it is already listed as a stretch goal in Section 13 of your plan, and it turns a potential weakness in your methodology into a demonstrated strength.

03 — Making the estimate stable

Welch's method, and the variance you didn't know you had

There is a problem with computing that ratio from a single FFT, and it is subtle enough that most people never notice.

A raw spectrum from one FFT (called a periodogram) is a noisy estimate. Not noisy because your signal is noisy, but noisy in itself: even with infinite data, each bin's value jitters around its true value and never settles down. Take a longer recording and you get more bins, each still just as jittery.

That jitter propagates straight into your quality score, which will jump around from window to window even when conditions are perfectly stable. And a quality score that jumps randomly makes your fusion weights jump randomly, which is worse than no adaptation at all.

Welch's method fixes it. Chop the signal into overlapping segments, FFT each one, and average the resulting spectra. Random jitter averages away; genuine peaks reinforce.

Welch trades resolution for stability. Shorter segments mean coarser frequency resolution but a calmer estimate. Since your quality metric needs a reliable floor more than it needs fine resolution, this is a trade worth making for the metric — while still using the full-length FFT for the BPM readout itself.

In practice that means running two spectral computations per window, which sounds wasteful but costs almost nothing:

# full-length FFT — best resolution, for reading the BPM freqs, power = periodogram(x, fs) bpm = freqs[argmax(power[band])] * 60 # Welch — stable floor, for judging quality wf, wp = welch(x, fs, nperseg=len(x)//4) quality = bandRatio(wf, wp)

04 — The payoff

Weighting three methods by how much you trust them

You now have a trust score for every method. Fusion is what you do with them, and it is deliberately simple arithmetic:

q_green, q_chrom, q_pos ← quality scores, each 0 to 1 w_i = q_i^p / Σ q_j^p ← normalise so weights sum to 1 # fuse in the frequency domain, not on the BPM numbers fusedPower = Σ w_i · power_i bpm = freqs[argmax(fusedPower[band])] * 60

Two design choices worth defending in your report

The sharpness exponent p. At p = 1 weights are proportional to quality, which is gentle: a method scoring 0.3 still contributes meaningfully. Raising p concentrates weight on the leaders, and as p grows large the system converges on simply picking the single best method each frame. Your fusion claim is that a blend beats winner-take-all, so p is exactly the knob that claim lives on, and showing accuracy against p is a genuine result rather than a parameter you tuned quietly.

Fuse spectra, not BPM numbers. This is the more important choice. If two methods disagree, 70 and 140, averaging their BPM gives 105, which is a rate neither method saw and the subject does not have. Averaging their spectra keeps both peaks present and lets the strongest survive the final argmax. Averaging in the wrong domain can produce an answer that is worse than either input, and that is a real trap.

The honest risk in your project, stated plainly

Fusion does not always beat the best single method. If one method is reliably best across all your test conditions, a fixed choice wins and your fusion adds complexity for nothing. Fusion pays off precisely when different methods win under different conditions, so no single fixed choice is right everywhere.

That is why the video-call compression testing in Section 5.1 of your plan matters so much: it deliberately creates conditions where the ranking flips. Design your evaluation to include conditions where the winner changes, or your headline result may honestly come out as "fusion roughly matched POS," which is a much weaker paper.

05 — Beyond the spectrum

Three more quality signals worth fusing in

Spectral SNR is your primary metric, but it has a blind spot: it judges the output without looking at the input. A completely obscured face can still produce a spuriously peaky spectrum by chance. Cheap independent checks catch that.

SignalHow to compute itWhat it catches
Motion magnitudeFrame-to-frame displacement of the tracked face boxTalking, turning, fidgeting — the exact failure mode of the green channel
Illumination stabilityVariance of mean ROI brightness over the windowFlicker, someone walking past a lamp, screens changing behind the subject
Temporal consistencyHow far this window's BPM sits from the last fewHarmonic lock-ons and sudden implausible jumps

Combine them multiplicatively, so that any one of them going bad is enough to suppress a method:

q_total = q_spectral × q_motion × q_illumination × q_consistency

Multiplication rather than averaging is the right choice here. With an average, a method with a beautiful spectrum but wild motion still scores respectably. With multiplication, one near-zero factor drags the whole score to near zero, which is the behaviour you want from a trust score.

06 — Reference

Tier 3 vocabulary, plainly

Power
Magnitude squared. Positive, additive, and weights strong components more.
Power spectrum
The ordinary spectrum with every value squared. What you sum over to measure energy.
Parseval's theorem
Total energy is the same whether you measure it in time or in frequency. Lets you ask "what fraction is in this band?" using bins alone.
PSD
Power spectral density: power per unit frequency, so the number does not change when you alter your bin spacing.
Periodogram
The power spectrum from a single FFT. Unbiased but permanently noisy.
Welch's method
Split into overlapping segments, FFT each, average the results. Calmer estimate, coarser resolution.
Variance of an estimate
How much a computed number jitters between runs on equivalent data. Different from noise in the signal, and easy to confuse with it.
Spectral SNR
In-band peak energy divided by total in-band energy. Your quality metric.
Fusion weight
How much a method's output counts toward the final answer. Derived from its quality score and normalised so the weights sum to 1.
Winner-take-all
Giving all the weight to the highest-scoring method. What TRACE becomes as the sharpness exponent grows large.
07 — Before Tier 4

Check yourself

Conditions are stable but your quality score bounces between 0.4 and 0.7 window to window. What is happening?

You are measuring the variance of the periodogram, not a change in your signal. A single-FFT spectrum estimate is inherently jittery, and your metric is faithfully reporting that jitter.

Use Welch for the quality computation. If the bouncing persists, add temporal smoothing to the score itself — an exponential moving average over the last few windows — since real recording conditions change over seconds, not milliseconds.

Two methods report 70 and 140 BPM with roughly equal quality. Why must you not average those numbers?

Because you would output 105 BPM, a value neither method produced and the subject does not have. Averaging is only meaningful when the inputs are estimates of the same thing with independent errors, and here one of them has almost certainly locked onto a harmonic.

Fuse the spectra instead. Both peaks stay present in the combined spectrum, and the final argmax picks a rate that at least one method actually observed. Better still, run the sub-harmonic check from Tier 1: the presence of 70 with strong energy is direct evidence that 140 is the harmonic.

Why include the harmonic in the numerator of the quality metric?

Because a real pulse waveform is sharp, and Tier 1 showed that sharp shapes require harmonics. Energy at twice the heart rate is evidence of a genuine pulse, not evidence of contamination.

Count it as noise and you systematically penalise the cleanest, sharpest pulse signals — the metric would score a crisp waveform worse than a mushy one, which is precisely backwards.

Your fusion result is almost identical to POS alone across every test. Is the project a failure?

No, but it is an incomplete evaluation. That result means POS dominated in every condition you tested, so there was nothing for adaptation to do.

The fix is to test conditions where the ranking actually flips: heavy compression, harsh or dim lighting, deliberate motion. That is exactly what Section 5.1 of your plan is for. And if fusion genuinely never wins, report that honestly with the conditions you tested — a negative result properly bounded is real science, and it reads far better than a result quietly tuned until it looked positive.
