00 — Orientation

Where this sits in your pipeline

Two of the eleven steps in your plan are filters:

  • Step 4, detrending. Remove the slow drift caused by lighting changes and the subject settling in their chair.
  • Step 6, bandpass filtering. Keep only 0.7 to 4 Hz, the range a human heart rate could plausibly occupy.

Both are convolutions, which Tier 1 covered. This tier is about choosing which convolution, because the defaults will hurt you in two specific ways: a filter that removes more than you intended, and a filter that shifts your signal in time without telling you.

That second one is the important one. Your project's headline extension is heart-rate variability, which is built entirely on when each beat happened. A filter that moves beats around corrupts the measurement while leaving the spectrum looking perfectly healthy, so nothing warns you.

01 — The simplest filter

A moving average, and what it does to your frequencies

Average each sample with its neighbours. That is a moving average, and it is the first filter anyone writes:

kernel = np.ones(K) / K smoothed = np.convolve(signal, kernel, mode="same")

Everyone knows it smooths. Almost nobody knows what it does in the frequency domain, and that is the half that matters, because Tier 1 established that filtering is reshaping a spectrum.

Its frequency response turns out to be a sinc shape: a broad main lobe that passes low frequencies, and a series of ripples that partially pass higher ones. It is a leaky, imprecise low-pass filter. It also has nulls, exact frequencies it deletes entirely, which can bite you badly if one lands on your heart rate.

Drag the kernel length and watch the response move.

Plain English

Frequency response means: for each frequency, how much of it survives the filter? A gain of 1 means untouched, 0 means deleted, 0.5 means halved. Plotting gain against frequency tells you everything a filter does, and it is the only honest way to judge one.

02 — Pipeline step 4

Detrending is a high-pass in disguise

Your plan describes detrending as "convolve with a moving-average kernel, then subtract." That is exactly right, and it is worth seeing why it works:

trend = convolve(signal, movingAverage) → keeps only the slow part detrended = signal − trend → keeps everything else

If the moving average keeps the slow stuff, then subtracting it keeps the fast stuff. Low-pass subtracted from the original gives you a high-pass. You built one filter out of another with a minus sign.

This step matters more than it looks. Raw skin-brightness signals are dominated by drift: the sun goes behind a cloud, the subject leans forward, a screen behind them changes colour. That drift is many times larger than the pulse, and if you FFT without removing it, an enormous low-frequency spike swamps the plot and its leakage skirts bury your heart rate.

Choose the detrend window with care

Too long and drift survives. Too short and the moving average starts tracking the pulse itself, so subtracting it removes your signal. A sensible rule is to make the window several times longer than one heartbeat: at 30 fps and 72 BPM, one beat is 25 samples, so a window of 45 to 90 samples removes drift while leaving the pulse untouched. Figure 1's null calculation is how you check that claim rather than trusting it.

03 — Pipeline step 6

Designing the 0.7 to 4 Hz bandpass

Now the main filter. You want to keep 0.7 to 4 Hz and reject everything else. The ideal version is a perfect rectangle: gain 1 inside the band, gain 0 outside, vertical walls.

You cannot have it. A perfectly rectangular frequency response requires an infinitely long kernel in time, and you do not have infinite data. Every real filter is a compromise, and filter design is the art of choosing which compromise.

04 — The trap

Filters move your signal in time

Every filter delays its input. That is unavoidable: a filter has to see some of the signal before it can respond to it.

If the filter delayed everything by the same amount you could simply subtract it and move on. The problem is that most filters delay different frequencies by different amounts. That is called non-linear phase, and it does something worse than shifting your signal. It changes its shape, because the components that make up the waveform arrive out of step with each other.

For measuring average BPM, none of this matters at all. The FFT throws phase away, so a distorted, shifted waveform gives the same spectrum peak.

For heart-rate variability it is fatal.

HRV is the measurement of tiny differences in beat timing — tens of milliseconds. A filter that moves beats by an amount that varies with the signal is injecting error directly into the quantity you are trying to measure.

Below, the same pulse train goes through the same filter twice: once normally, once with filtfilt. Watch the detected beat positions against the truth.

How filtfilt works, and why it is free

The trick is almost embarrassingly simple. Run the filter forwards, then reverse the result and run the same filter over it again, then reverse it back.

# wrong for HRV — introduces a frequency-dependent delay y = scipy.signal.lfilter(b, a, x) # correct — zero phase distortion y = scipy.signal.filtfilt(b, a, x)

The second pass applies exactly the same delay in the opposite direction, so the two cancel to precisely zero at every frequency. The magnitude response gets applied twice, which makes the filter effectively steeper and squares the gain, so account for that when picking your order. Everything else is free.

The one restriction: it needs the whole signal in advance, since it has to run backwards through it. That makes it perfect for offline analysis of your validation datasets, and unavailable for a strictly real-time stream. In practice your live demo processes a sliding window of the last 20 or 30 seconds, which is a complete block of data, so filtfilt works there too.

Put this in your report

"We used zero-phase filtering (filtfilt) throughout, because single-pass IIR filtering introduces a frequency-dependent group delay that would bias the inter-beat interval estimates underpinning our HRV metrics." That one sentence demonstrates you understood a subtlety most student projects never notice, and it costs you nothing to have got right.

05 — Reference

Tier 2 vocabulary, plainly

Frequency response
For each frequency, how much survives the filter. Gain 1 is untouched, 0 is deleted.
Passband
The range you are keeping. For you, 0.7 to 4 Hz.
Stopband
The range you are rejecting.
Transition width
How much frequency it takes to get from pass to stop. Narrow means steep means expensive.
Ripple
Wobble in the gain where it should be flat. A moving average has a lot of it.
Null
A frequency the filter deletes completely. A moving average has them at regular spacings, and one landing on your heart rate is a real hazard.
FIR
Finite impulse response. A fixed-length kernel you convolve with. Can have perfectly linear phase.
IIR
Infinite impulse response. Uses feedback, so it is very cheap and very steep, but its phase is not linear.
Butterworth
A common IIR design with a maximally flat passband. The usual choice for this kind of work.
Order
Roughly, how steep the filter is. Higher order means sharper edges and more phase distortion.
Group delay
How long a given frequency is held up by the filter. Constant across frequency is good; varying is what wrecks beat timing.
Linear phase
Every frequency delayed by the same amount, so the waveform shifts without changing shape.
Zero-phase / filtfilt
Filtering forwards then backwards so the delays cancel exactly. Mandatory before any timing measurement.
Detrending
Subtracting a smoothed copy from the original. A high-pass built out of a low-pass.
06 — Before Tier 3

Check yourself

Your BPM readings look fine but your RMSSD values are wildly too large. What do you check first?

Whether you used lfilter instead of filtfilt. The pattern is diagnostic: BPM is unaffected because the FFT discards phase, while HRV is destroyed because it is made entirely of timing.

Second suspect: peak detection resolution. If you are locating beats to the nearest whole frame at 30 fps, your timing quantum is 33 ms, which is already comparable to the RMSSD you are trying to measure. That is what parabolic interpolation, in Tier 4, exists to fix.

You widen your detrending window to remove drift more thoroughly, and your pulse gets weaker. Why?

You have it backwards. A wider moving average tracks only slower things, so it removes drift better and leaves the pulse alone. If widening the window is weakening the pulse, check the sign of your change: you have probably narrowed it, so the moving average is now fast enough to follow the heartbeat itself, and subtracting it removes the signal.

Figure 1 gives you the exact check. Compute where the first null lands for your kernel length and confirm it sits well above 4 Hz.

Why does raising the low cutoff from 0.7 to 1.4 Hz cause the 144 BPM bug?

A resting heart rate of 72 BPM is 1.2 Hz, which now sits below your cutoff and gets attenuated. Its second harmonic at 2.4 Hz sits comfortably inside the passband and passes untouched.

You have therefore built a filter that suppresses the fundamental while preserving the harmonic, which is precisely the condition where argmax picks the wrong peak. The filter did not reveal a problem in the data; it manufactured one.
