01 — The trap

Two systems, identical error, completely different problems

Almost every student project reports one number: mean absolute error. Here is why that is not enough.

Imagine two versions of your system, both reporting an MAE of 3.0 BPM.

  • System A is unbiased and a bit noisy. Sometimes 3 too high, sometimes 3 too low, averaging out correctly.
  • System B is precise but reads 3 BPM too high every single time.

These are not equally good. System A is a decent measurement with room to improve by averaging. System B has a calibration fault: it would tell every user their heart rate is elevated, and no amount of averaging fixes it. Yet MAE scores them identically.

Worse, some faults only appear at part of the range. A system can be perfect at 60 BPM and read 10 too low at 120, which is exactly the failure you would care about most, and MAE dilutes it into a modest-looking average.

MAE tells you how big your errors are. It never tells you what shape they are. Shape is where the diagnosis lives.
Plain English

Bias is your average error with its sign kept. MAE throws the signs away by taking absolute values, so +3 and −3 both become 3 and cancel nothing. Bias keeps them, so consistent over-reading shows up as a positive number and randomness averages to near zero. That one change of definition is what separates the two systems above.

02 — The standard tool

Bland-Altman: the plot medical papers actually use

When a paper compares a new measurement method against an established one, this is the plot it shows. Dasari et al. (2021), already in your reading list, uses it to evaluate CHROM and POS against an FDA-approved sensor, so you have a worked example in your own field to copy the layout from.

The construction is almost too simple:

for each paired measurement: x-axis = (yours + truth) / 2 ← the average, i.e. "roughly what rate was this?" y-axis = yours − truth ← the difference, signed then draw three horizontal lines: bias = mean(differences) upper limit = bias + 1.96 × sd(differences) lower limit = bias − 1.96 × sd(differences)

Those limits of agreement are where 95 percent of your errors fall. They let you make a genuinely useful statement: "in 95 percent of measurements, our estimate lands within 4.2 BPM of the reference." That is a claim a reader can act on, in a way that "MAE was 3.0" is not.

And the plot's shape diagnoses the fault directly:

What you seeWhat it means
Cloud centred on zero, no patternHealthy. Random error only.
Cloud centred above or below zeroConstant bias. Fixable by calibration.
Cloud tiltedProportional bias: error depends on the rate. The serious one.
Cloud fanning outErrors grow at higher rates. Report the limits separately per range.
Where Pearson r misleads you

Correlation measures whether two things move together, not whether they agree. A system reading exactly 20 BPM too high correlates at r = 1.00 while being wrong on every single measurement.

Report r, because every paper in your field does and it makes your table comparable. But never let it carry the accuracy claim on its own. Agreement is Bland-Altman's job.

Those are illustrative figures, not predictions. Three rules for filling it in honestly:

  1. Report a spread, never a single number. Standard deviation across subjects, or a 95 percent confidence interval. One lucky recording is not a result.
  2. Include the ablation. Each component on and off. This is what defends the fusion claim with evidence rather than assertion, and it is already Section 10 of your plan.
  3. Report the conditions where you lose. A table where your method wins every row invites suspicion. A table with one row where POS beats you, honestly reported and briefly explained, is more credible than a clean sweep.

04 — The physical cause

Why a video call attacks exactly what rPPG needs

Section 5.1 of your plan tests robustness under Zoom-style degradation. That experiment rests on one fact about how video codecs work, and knowing it turns your experiment from empirical poking into a targeted hypothesis.

Human vision is far more sensitive to brightness than to colour. Codec designers exploit this ruthlessly through chroma subsampling: store brightness at full resolution, and colour at a quarter of it.

4:4:4 full colour resolution — rare; some professional formats 4:2:2 colour halved horizontally — broadcast 4:2:0 colour quartered — H.264, VP8, VP9: everything you will meet

Now recall what your signal actually is. Tier 0 showed that the pulse lives in the difference between colour channels, because hemoglobin absorbs green much more strongly than red. That is chroma information, precisely.

Video compression preserves the channel rPPG does not need and discards the channel it lives in. Chroma subsampling is close to a worst-case attack on rPPG, and it is running on every video call in the world.

Quantisation compounds it. The pulse changes skin colour by well under one percent, so its amplitude is a fraction of a single pixel value. Coarse quantisation at low bitrate rounds those fractions to zero. The signal is not degraded, it is deleted.

What this predicts for your experiment

You can now state hypotheses before running anything, which is a much stronger way to write a Method section:

  • Methods relying on chrominance differences (CHROM, POS) should degrade fastest under aggressive subsampling.
  • The naive green channel, being closer to raw brightness, may degrade more gracefully under compression even though it loses badly on motion. If your data shows that, it is a genuinely interesting finding and not a bug.
  • That reversal is exactly the condition where adaptive fusion should win, because the ranking of methods flips between conditions and no static choice is right in both.

That last point is worth dwelling on. Tier 3 warned that fusion only pays off when different methods win under different conditions. Compression may be the condition that creates the flip your project needs, which makes Section 5.1 not just a robustness test but the experiment your headline claim depends on.

05 — Reference

Tier 5 vocabulary, plainly

MAE
Mean absolute error. Average size of your mistakes, ignoring direction.
RMSE
Root mean square error. Like MAE but squares first, so it punishes large mistakes harder. Always at least as big as MAE.
Bias
Mean error keeping the sign. Reveals consistent over- or under-reading that MAE conceals.
Pearson r
How well two measurements move together. Can be 1.00 while agreement is terrible.
Bland-Altman
Difference plotted against average, with bias and 95% limits drawn. The standard agreement plot in medical measurement.
Limits of agreement
Bias ± 1.96 standard deviations. The range containing 95% of your errors.
Proportional bias
Error that grows with the value being measured. Shows as a tilt in Bland-Altman and is invisible in MAE.
Ablation study
Turning each component off in turn to show how much it contributed. What defends a fusion claim.
Confidence interval
A range that likely contains the true value. Report one instead of a bare number.
Luma / chroma
Brightness and colour, stored separately by every video codec so colour can be discarded more aggressively.
Chroma subsampling
Storing colour at lower resolution than brightness. 4:2:0 keeps a quarter, and is the default everywhere.
Quantisation
Rounding pixel values coarsely to save bits. Rounds a sub-one-percent pulse signal straight to zero.
Bitrate
Bits per second the codec is allowed. The main knob controlling how much gets thrown away.
06 — Last check

Check yourself

Your MAE is 2.1 BPM and r is 0.98. Your supervisor is unconvinced. What are they worried about?

Systematic bias, which neither number can rule out. A system reading consistently 2 BPM high produces exactly those figures while being wrong on every measurement, and r would stay near 1.00 even at a 20 BPM offset.

Show a Bland-Altman plot with the bias and limits of agreement stated. Also show the spread across subjects, since a good mean can hide one subject the system fails on entirely.

Why might the naive green channel beat CHROM at very low bitrates?

Because CHROM works on differences between colour channels, and chroma subsampling is precisely the operation that destroys colour detail. The green channel sits closer to the luma information the codec goes out of its way to protect.

If your experiment shows this, it is a real finding worth reporting prominently rather than an anomaly to explain away. It also happens to be the strongest possible evidence for your fusion claim, because it is a documented condition where the best method changes.

Your Bland-Altman cloud tilts downward: accurate at 60 BPM, reading low at 120. What is one likely cause?

Check your filter's upper edge first. If the bandpass rolls off before 2 Hz, the fundamental of a fast heart rate is being attenuated while its harmonic is not, so at high rates the peak-picking becomes unreliable in exactly one direction. Tier 2's Figure 3 shows this happening.

Second candidate: window length. Faster rates fit more cycles into a fixed window, so the resolution behaves differently across the range and any harmonic confusion shows up asymmetrically.

Why report a condition where your method loses?

Because a table where the proposed method wins every row is the signature of selective reporting, and experienced readers discount it automatically. Every real method has a regime where something else is better.

Naming that regime shows you understand your own method's mechanism rather than just its score, and it makes your winning rows more believable, not less. Reviewers trust papers that know their own limits.

07 — Done

All five tiers

What you can now build, end to end

A camera samples skin brightness 30 times a second (Tier 1). You project R, G and B onto a direction chosen so lighting artifacts land perpendicular and vanish (Tier 0). You detrend with a subtracted moving average and bandpass with a zero-phase filter that does not move your beats (Tier 2).

You wind the result around a circle at every candidate rate, and the frequency where the dots pile up lopsided is the heart rate (Tier 0). You check its sub-harmonic, because a sharp pulse produces harmonics and the tallest spike is not always the right one (Tier 1).

You measure what fraction of the band's energy sits in that peak, and that fraction becomes a trust score which weights three methods against each other, frame by frame (Tier 3).

You locate each beat to sub-frame precision, build the interval series, resample it onto an even grid because it is not evenly spaced by construction, and transform it a second time to read the LF and HF bands (Tier 4).

Then you prove all of it against ground truth with bias, limits of agreement, and an ablation, and you test it under the compression a real telehealth call would apply (Tier 5).

None of it uses a trained model. Every number traces back to a convolution or a Fourier transform. That is the claim Section 17 of your plan makes, and you can now defend every line of it.
