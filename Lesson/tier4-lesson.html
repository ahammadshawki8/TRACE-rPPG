The Second Transform

00 — Orientation

Why this is the most impressive part of your project

Every other team's demo will end with a number on screen. Yours goes one step further, and the step is genuinely elegant rather than merely more work:

You take the Fourier transform twice. Once on the video signal, to find the heartbeats. Then again on the pattern of gaps between those heartbeats, to read the state of the nervous system controlling them.

The second transform operates on a completely different signal from the first. Its input is not brightness over time; it is interval length over beat number. Same mathematics, entirely different physical meaning, and the output is a recognised clinical measure rather than something you invented.

Getting there needs four things, and every one of them is a place people go wrong:

  1. Locating beats to far better precision than one video frame.
  2. Turning beat times into an interval series.
  3. Handling the fact that this series is not evenly sampled, which breaks the FFT's core assumption.
  4. Recording for long enough to see the slow rhythms at all — and your plan currently does not.
01 — Sub-frame precision

Your frame rate is not your timing resolution

At 30 fps your frames are 33.3 ms apart. If you locate each beat at the nearest frame, every beat time carries up to 16 ms of rounding error.

Now consider what you are measuring. RMSSD, the standard short-term HRV metric, is typically 20 to 50 ms in a healthy adult. Your rounding error is the same size as the signal. Measuring in whole frames is like weighing a letter on bathroom scales.

The fix is a genuinely small amount of code. A peak in a sampled signal is a smooth curve that was measured at discrete points, so the true maximum almost never lands exactly on a sample. Take the peak sample and its two neighbours, fit a parabola through those three points, and solve for where that curve peaks.

# y1, y2, y3 = the three samples, y2 being the highest delta = 0.5 * (y1 - y3) / (y1 - 2*y2 + y3) truePeakIndex = peakIndex + delta # delta is between -0.5 and +0.5

Three lines, and your timing precision improves by roughly an order of magnitude. Drag the true peak below and watch the two estimates diverge.

02 — Building the series

From beats to intervals

Once you have precise beat times, the interval series is a subtraction:

beats = [0.83, 1.68, 2.49, 3.35, 4.14, ...] # seconds RR = np.diff(beats) * 1000 # milliseconds = [850, 810, 860, 790, ...]

Those numbers are the raw material for everything that follows. Note immediately what they show: the intervals are not constant. A healthy heart never beats like a metronome, and that variation is the signal, not noise. A perfectly regular heartbeat is a sign of illness, not health.

Three standard time-domain metrics come straight out of this list:

MetricWhat it computesWhat it reflects
SDNNStandard deviation of all the intervalsOverall variability across the whole recording
RMSSDRoot mean square of successive differencesFast beat-to-beat changes; mostly parasympathetic
pNN50Percentage of successive pairs differing by over 50 msSimilar to RMSSD, easier to explain out loud

Before computing any of them, clean the series. A single missed or doubled beat creates an interval of roughly double or half the surrounding ones, and because RMSSD squares differences, one bad interval can inflate it enormously. Reject any interval more than about 20 percent from its neighbours, and say in your report that you did.

03 — The trap

You cannot FFT the interval series directly

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes, and it produces plausible-looking numbers that are wrong.

# WRONG spectrum = np.fft.rfft(RR)

Every figure in Tier 0 assumed samples arrive at evenly spaced instants. The winding machine assigns each sample an angle based on when it happened, so even spacing is not decorative, it is load-bearing.

Your RR series violates this by construction. Sample number 5 is the interval after the fifth beat, and beats do not arrive on a regular schedule. That irregularity is literally the quantity you are measuring. The x-axis of your RR series is beat number, not time, and an FFT of it is answering a question about beat index that has no clean physical meaning.

There are two accepted fixes.

Resample onto an even gridLomb-Scargle periodogram
IdeaInterpolate the intervals onto a regular 4 Hz time base, then FFT normallyA method built to handle unevenly spaced data directly

04 — The payoff

LF, HF, and what the ratio actually means

With a properly resampled interval series, take its FFT. Two bands are conventionally defined, from the 1996 Task Force standard already on your reading list:

BandRangeWhat drives it
LF0.04 – 0.15 HzA mix of sympathetic and parasympathetic activity, plus blood-pressure regulation
HF0.15 – 0.40 HzParasympathetic activity, tightly coupled to breathing

The HF band deserves a moment, because it makes the whole thing concrete. Its range corresponds to 9 to 24 breaths per minute, which is exactly normal breathing. Your heart genuinely speeds up slightly as you inhale and slows as you exhale, an effect called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. So the HF peak in your plot is the subject's breathing, visible in their heartbeat, extracted from a video of their face. That is a striking thing to be able to point at during a demo.

Stay honest about what LF/HF means

LF/HF is often described as "sympathetic versus parasympathetic balance." That interpretation is contested in the current literature, because LF is not purely sympathetic. Your plan already gets this right by framing the output as a wellness indicator with an explicit non-diagnostic caveat.

Keep that discipline, and consider going one better: state in your Discussion that the simple sympathovagal reading is disputed, and that you report the ratio as a relative indicator rather than a physiological claim. Knowing the limitations of your own metric is exactly the kind of thing that separates a strong report from a competent one.

05 — Fix this in your plan

Thirty seconds is not enough for LF

Section 7 step 1 of your plan says "capture 20 to 30 seconds of face video." For BPM that is a sensible choice. For LF/HF it does not work, and the arithmetic is unforgiving.

LF band starts at 0.04 Hz one cycle of 0.04 Hz takes 25 seconds in a 30-second window you capture 1.2 cycles → you cannot measure a rhythm you have barely seen once

Tier 0 established that resolution is 1/T. At 30 seconds that is 0.033 Hz, and the entire LF band is only 0.11 Hz wide. You would be describing the whole band with about three bins, two of which are contaminated by the edges.

The 1996 Task Force standard specifies 5-minute recordings for short-term LF/HF, and that is not a conservative safety margin, it is roughly the minimum for a stable estimate.

Your demo needs two capture modes: a live 20 to 30 second window that continuously updates BPM, and a separate longer "HRV reading" of at least two minutes, ideally five, that the subject sits still for.

This is good news for your demo rather than bad. It gives the presentation a natural two-act structure: the instant live readout for the "wait, how?" moment, then a deliberate stillness while the deeper measurement accumulates. That is a more interesting thing to watch than a single number ticking, and it accurately reflects how real clinical HRV measurement is done.

One concrete change to make now: update Section 7 step 1 and Section 5.2 of your plan to specify the two capture modes and cite the Task Force 5-minute standard as the justification. Better to have designed it that way than to discover it when the LF/HF number refuses to settle.

Tier 4 vocabulary, plainly

RR interval
The gap between consecutive heartbeats, in milliseconds. Named for the R wave of an ECG.
IBI
Inter-beat interval. The same thing, used when the source is not an ECG. Your papers will use both words.
HRV
Heart rate variability. The study of how those gaps fluctuate. High variability is generally healthy.
SDNN
Standard deviation of the intervals. Overall variability.
RMSSD
Root mean square of successive differences. Captures fast beat-to-beat changes. Typically 20 to 50 ms.
pNN50
Percentage of consecutive interval pairs differing by more than 50 ms.
Parabolic interpolation
Fitting a curve through three samples to locate a peak between them. Three lines of code, roughly ten times better timing.
Uneven sampling
Samples not equally spaced in time. Breaks the FFT's core assumption. Unavoidable in an interval series.
Lomb-Scargle
A periodogram designed for unevenly spaced data. Borrowed from astronomy, where observations happen when the telescope is free.
LF band
0.04 to 0.15 Hz. Mixed autonomic influences and blood-pressure regulation.
HF band
0.15 to 0.40 Hz. Parasympathetic, and tied directly to breathing.
RSA
Respiratory sinus arrhythmia: heart rate rising on inhale and falling on exhale. The physical cause of the HF peak.
LF/HF ratio
A widely reported balance indicator whose simple interpretation is contested. Report it, and say so.
07 — Before Tier 5

Check yourself

Your RMSSD comes out around 90 ms when you expected roughly 40. Name two likely causes.

A missed or doubled beat. RMSSD squares successive differences, so one bad interval contributes enormously. Apply outlier rejection before computing anything, and inspect the interval series by eye during development.

Whole-frame peak detection. At 30 fps, rounding to the nearest frame injects up to 16 ms of random error into every beat time, and RMSSD is precisely a measure of beat-to-beat jitter, so it absorbs that error directly. Use parabolic interpolation.

Also worth ruling out: single-pass filtering instead of filtfilt, from Tier 2, which shifts beats by a frequency-dependent amount.

Why can't you just FFT the RR series directly?

Because its x-axis is beat number, not time, and those two are only the same thing if the heart beats perfectly regularly — which would mean zero HRV, the exact opposite of what you are measuring.

The FFT would place sample 5 at time 5 units, when it actually occurred at whatever moment the fifth beat happened. Every dot goes onto the winding circle at slightly the wrong angle, smearing the result. Resample onto a uniform 4 Hz grid first.

Your teacher asks what the HF peak physically represents. What do you say?

It is the subject's breathing. Heart rate rises slightly on inhalation and falls on exhalation, an effect called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, so the breathing rhythm shows up directly in the beat intervals.

You can prove it live: ask them to breathe slowly and deliberately, and the HF peak visibly moves to a lower frequency. That is a strong demo moment, because it is a verifiable prediction rather than an assertion, and it takes ten seconds.

Why won't a 30-second recording give a usable LF/HF ratio?

LF starts at 0.04 Hz, whose period is 25 seconds. Thirty seconds contains barely one cycle, and you cannot characterise a rhythm you have seen once.

In resolution terms, 1/30 s gives 0.033 Hz bins while the entire LF band is 0.11 Hz wide, so you would describe it with about three bins. The Task Force standard specifies 5 minutes. Build a separate longer capture mode for the HRV reading and keep the short window for live BPM.
