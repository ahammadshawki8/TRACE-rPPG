Parallel or Fanning

Your project stopped being a system you build and became a question you answer. This is how to answer it so that a reviewer believes you.

00 — Orientation

What changed

Until now the project was: build a signal-quality-adaptive fusion system, validate it, show it works. That is a good course project.

The literature search changed the target. Four of the five things you planned to claim are already published, some of them within the last year. What remains open is not a system at all. It is a question nobody has measured:

Does video compression amplify the skin-tone accuracy gap in rPPG?

Both halves are known separately. Darker skin gives a weaker pulse signal because melanin absorbs more light. Compression destroys weak colour detail. Nobody has asked whether the second makes the first worse.

That changes what you are building toward. You are no longer writing a systems paper. You are writing a measurement paper, and measurement papers live or die on experimental design rather than on engineering.

So this tier is about design and evidence, not about signals. Your TRACE pipeline is still built exactly as planned. It just becomes the instrument rather than the finding.

Plain English

A measurement paper does not propose a new method. It measures something carefully that nobody measured before, and reports the number honestly. Many of the most cited papers in any field are this kind. They are also the right size for a poster.

Part A

The idea everything rests on

01 — The core concept

Main effects, and why they are not enough

Suppose you run your experiment and report two findings:

  • Lower bitrate makes accuracy worse. (an effect of compression)
  • Darker skin makes accuracy worse. (an effect of skin tone)

Both are true. Both are already published. Reporting them adds nothing, and a reviewer will say so.

Those two are called main effects: what each factor does on its own, averaged over the other. Your claim is not about either of them. It is about a third thing:

An interaction is when the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. Compression hurting darker skin more than it hurts lighter skin is an interaction, and it is a separate claim from either main effect.

The visual test is simple enough to state in one line, and it is why this page is called what it is:

  • Parallel curves → no interaction. Compression hurts everyone equally. The gap is constant.
  • Fanning curves → interaction. Compression hurts one group more. The gap grows.

Drag the sliders below until this is second nature. Set the interaction to zero and the lines stay parallel no matter how large you make either main effect. That is the whole point.

Why this matters more than it sounds

It is easy to run the experiment, see both curves going up as bitrate drops, and conclude you found something. You did not. You found two things already known.

The number you must extract, report, and defend is the size of the fan: how much larger the gap is at low bitrate than at high bitrate. That single quantity is your contribution.

02 — Writing it down

The model, in one line

Statisticians write the same idea as an equation. It is less frightening than it looks, and you will need it in the abstract.

error = β₀ + β₁·(bitrate) + β₂·(skin tone) + β₃·(bitrate × skin tone) + noise ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ baseline main effect main effect THE INTERACTION your entire contribution

The last term is a product of the two factors, which is exactly what "depends on the level of the other" means arithmetically. If β₃ is zero, the lines are parallel. If it is not, they fan.

So your poster's headline result reduces to three numbers:

  1. The estimate of β₃ — how big the fan is.
  2. A confidence interval on β₃ — how sure you are.
  3. Whether that interval includes zero — if it does, you cannot claim the effect exists.

In Python this is a few lines. statsmodels will fit it and hand you all three, and the formula syntax mirrors the equation almost exactly.

# the * operator expands to both main effects AND their interaction model = smf.ols("mae ~ bitrate * skin_group", data=df).fit() print(model.summary()) print(model.conf_int()) # the interval that decides your claim
Plain English

A confidence interval is the range your true value probably sits in. "The gap widened by 4.1 BPM, 95% CI [1.8, 6.4]" means: the best estimate is 4.1, and values from 1.8 to 6.4 are all plausible. Because that range does not include zero, you can say an effect exists. If it read [-0.9, 6.2], zero is plausible and you cannot.

Part B

Designing it

03 — The grid

Every cell must be filled

An interaction can only be measured if you observe every combination. Miss a cell and the term is not estimable, which is a fancy way of saying the analysis simply will not run.

Your grid has three dimensions:

DimensionLevelsNotes
Skin toneFitzpatrick I–III vs IV–VITwo groups is the minimum. Finer is better if subject counts allow.
Bitrate5 levels + lossless controlSpread across a realistic telehealth range. The control is essential.
Methodgreen, CHROM, POS, TRACE, 1 neuralThe neural baseline is now required, not optional.

That is 2 × 6 × 5 = 60 cells, each needing several subjects. Explore it below and see how quickly the requirement grows.

Use the same subjects at every bitrate

Do not record different people at different bitrates. Pass the same recordings through every compression level, so each subject acts as their own control. This removes person-to-person variation from the comparison entirely and makes a much smaller sample sufficient. It is called a within-subject design, and it is the single biggest gift available to you here.

04 — The threat

Your source video must be clean

This is the most likely way the whole study fails, so it gets its own section.

If your source video has already been compressed, then your "high bitrate" condition is not a clean baseline. It is already damaged. Re-encoding it adds a second round of loss on top of the first, and the two interact in ways you cannot separate afterwards.

This matters immediately, because MMPD, the obvious dataset for skin tone, ships compressed to 320×240. It cannot serve as your primary source.

What to use instead

  1. VitalVideo — 893 subjects across all six Fitzpatrick types. Confirm access terms and source quality first; this is the strongest option if it is clean.
  2. Your own recordings — recruiting Fitzpatrick IV to V participants in Dhaka is genuinely easier for you than for most labs, and a pulse oximeter costs very little. Needs consent and an ethics statement.
  3. UBFC-rPPG — uncompressed and free, but roughly 5 percent dark-skinned subjects, so it cannot carry the skin-tone axis. Useful as a clean control.

Whatever you choose, state the source codec and bitrate in the poster. A reviewer who knows this field will look for it, and its absence reads as carelessness.

Part C

Believing the result

05 — Enough subjects

Interactions need more data than main effects

Here is an uncomfortable rule that catches many first studies:

Detecting an interaction reliably takes roughly four times as many subjects as detecting a main effect of the same size.

The reason is intuitive once seen. A main effect is a difference between two averages. An interaction is a difference between two differences, so it accumulates uncertainty from four groups instead of two. More uncertainty in, more data needed to see through it.

This is why you must decide your sample size before collecting, not after. Running an underpowered study and finding nothing tells you nothing: you cannot distinguish "no effect exists" from "my study was too small to see it."

Plain English

Effect size means how big the difference is relative to how noisy the data is. A large effect in clean data is easy to see with few subjects. A small effect in noisy data needs many. Your within-subject design from Section 03 reduces noise substantially, which is exactly why it lets you get away with fewer people.

06 — Reading your own plot

Is that fan real, or is it noise?

You will get your plot. Two curves will separate somewhat. The question is whether you are looking at a finding or at randomness, and eyes are unreliable judges of this.

The honest way to present it is with confidence bands around each curve. If the bands overlap everywhere, the separation you think you see is not supported. If they pull apart at low bitrate, you have something.

Below is simulated data. Change the sample size and watch the bands tighten around exactly the same underlying truth.

Set the true interaction to zero and look again

With a genuinely zero effect, small samples will still occasionally produce an interval that excludes zero, and it will look like a discovery. That is a false positive, and it is why you commit to one analysis in advance rather than trying several and reporting the one that worked. Deciding the analysis after seeing the data is the most common way honest people produce wrong results.

07 — The other outcome

A negative result is still a result

The curves may come out parallel. Compression may hurt all skin tones equally. Plan for this now, because how you react to it decides whether you have a poster or nothing.

A clean negative finding here is genuinely worth reporting, for three reasons:

  • It contradicts a reasonable prior. The mechanism predicts amplification; showing it does not happen is informative.
  • It has practical consequence. It would mean bandwidth policy in telehealth is not an equity issue, which is worth knowing before someone assumes otherwise.
  • Nobody has measured it either way, so either answer is new.

The protection is to write your abstract so both outcomes are reportable. Phrase the contribution as "we measure whether..." rather than "we show that...". Then you cannot be wrong-footed by your own data.

A study designed so that only one outcome is publishable is a study under pressure to find that outcome. Design for either answer, and your result becomes trustworthy in a way that a positive-only design never is.
08 — Reference

Tier 6 vocabulary, plainly

Main effect
What one factor does on its own, averaged across the other. Both of yours are already published.
Interaction
When the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other. Parallel lines mean none; fanning lines mean one exists.
Factor
An input you deliberately vary. Yours are bitrate, skin tone group, and method.
Level
One setting of a factor. 500 kbps is a level of the bitrate factor.
Cell
One combination of levels across all factors. Every cell needs data or the interaction cannot be estimated.
Control condition
The clean, undamaged baseline you compare everything against. Here, lossless source video.
Confound
Something that varies alongside your factor and could explain the result instead. Pre-compressed source is yours.
Within-subject
The same people appear at every level, acting as their own control. Removes person-to-person variation and shrinks the sample you need.
Effect size
How big a difference is relative to the noise around it. Large effects need fewer subjects.
Statistical power
The chance your study finds a real effect. Convention is 0.80. Below that, absence of evidence means very little.
Underpowered
Too small to detect the effect you are looking for. Finding nothing then tells you nothing.
Confidence interval
The plausible range for the true value. If it contains zero, you cannot claim an effect exists.
β₃
The interaction coefficient. The size of the fan. Your entire contribution reduces to this number and its interval.
Two-way ANOVA
The standard test for two factors plus their interaction. Fit it with statsmodels in a few lines.
False positive
Concluding an effect exists when it does not. Small samples and analyses chosen after seeing the data both make this likelier.
Measurement paper
A paper whose contribution is a careful measurement rather than a new method. The right size for a poster.
09 — Before you start

Check yourself

Your plot shows both curves rising steeply as bitrate falls. Have you found your effect?

Not yet. Both curves rising is the main effect of compression, which is already published, plus the main effect of skin tone if one curve sits above the other throughout.

Your finding requires the vertical gap between the curves to be larger at low bitrate than at high bitrate. Measure that difference, put a confidence interval on it, and check whether the interval excludes zero. Rising curves alone are not a result.

Why can't you use MMPD as your primary dataset?

Because its videos ship already compressed to 320×240. Your high-bitrate condition would not be a clean baseline, so you would be measuring compression on top of compression with no way to separate the two afterwards.

Figure 3 shows the consequence: the measured fan comes out smaller than the real one, so you could conclude there is no effect when there is. Use VitalVideo if its source is clean, or record your own.

You have 6 subjects per group and find nothing. What can you conclude?

Almost nothing, and this is the important part. At that size your power to detect a moderate interaction is well below 0.50, so you were more likely to miss a real effect than to find it.

"We found no effect" and "our study was too small to see one" are indistinguishable from that data. Compute the required sample size before collecting, and if you cannot reach it, report the power you did have so readers can judge for themselves.

Your interval comes out [-0.4, 7.8]. The estimate is 3.7. Can you claim the effect?

No. The interval includes zero, so no effect at all remains plausible given your data, however encouraging 3.7 looks.

What you can honestly write is that your estimate points toward amplification but the study could not resolve it, and report the interval openly. That is a legitimate poster. Quietly dropping the interval and reporting only 3.7 is not.
