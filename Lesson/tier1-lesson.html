00 — Orientation

What changes here

Tier 0 gave you one machine: wind a signal around a circle at a test rhythm, and see whether the dots pile up. That machine is enough to find a heart rate in a clean laboratory recording.

It is not enough for a real one. Six things go wrong, and this tier is those six things:

  • Real pulses are not sine waves, so they produce more than one spike. Picking the biggest is not always picking the right one.
  • Filtering, which you will do constantly, is an operation called convolution, and you need to know what it actually does to a signal.
  • There is a shortcut for doing convolution through the FFT, and it has a trap at the edges that silently corrupts your data.
  • Your camera samples the world 30 times a second, and if it ever drops frames the assumption underneath every figure in Tier 0 quietly breaks.
  • The floor of your spectrum is never flat. It is lumpy, and that lumpiness has a name and a fix.
  • One popular trick looks like it improves your resolution and does not.
Part A

Fourier series

01 — Building blocks

Any repeating shape is a stack of sine waves

Tier 0 dealt with one rhythm at a time. Fourier series makes a bolder claim:

Take any shape that repeats. It can be built by adding together sine waves whose frequencies are all whole-number multiples of one base rate.

The base rate is the fundamental. The multiples are harmonics. A 1.2 Hz fundamental has harmonics at 2.4 Hz, 3.6 Hz, 4.8 Hz and so on.

This matters enormously to you, because a heartbeat is emphatically not a sine wave. It is a sharp upstroke, a fast fall, a small secondary bump, then a flat stretch until the next beat. To build a shape that sharp out of smooth sine waves, you need harmonics, and lots of them.

Add harmonics one at a time below and watch a rounded blob sharpen into something that looks like a real pulse.

Plain English

Fundamental is the actual repeat rate — the heart rate. Harmonic is an echo at a whole multiple of it: twice the rate, three times, and so on. They are not separate heartbeats. They are the mathematical price of the pulse shape being sharp rather than smooth.

02 — The bug you will hit

Why your system will report 144 BPM for a healthy adult

Here is the naive algorithm, and it is what almost every student project ships:

bpm = freqs[np.argmax(power)] * 60 # find the tallest spike

It works beautifully until it doesn't. The pulse has a spike at 72 BPM and a second spike at 144 BPM from the second harmonic. Normally the fundamental is taller and argmax picks correctly.

But the fundamental is the lowest frequency in your signal, which puts it right next to the slow lighting drift and breathing motion your filters are fighting. Aggressive high-pass filtering, poor lighting, or a subject whose pulse waveform is unusually sharp can all knock the fundamental down below its own harmonic. Then argmax confidently returns 144, and your demo tells your teacher that a calm person is in tachycardia.

Drag the slider below to weaken the fundamental and watch the naive answer flip.

Three ways to defend against it

  1. Search only inside a plausible band. You already restrict to 0.7 to 4 Hz. Tightening to something like 0.7 to 2.5 Hz when you have context about the subject removes many harmonic candidates outright.
  2. Check for a sub-harmonic. Before accepting a peak at frequency f, look at f/2. If there is meaningful energy there, you have probably locked onto a harmonic and the real answer is half your candidate.
  3. Enforce continuity across windows. Heart rate does not double between one second and the next. A tracker that rejects implausible jumps catches this almost for free, and it costs about ten lines.

Any of these is worth a paragraph in your Discussion section. Documenting a failure mode you anticipated and defended against reads far stronger than a results table with no failure analysis at all.

Part B

Convolution

03 — The operation

Convolution is flip, slide, multiply, sum

Every filter you will write is a convolution. Detrending is a convolution. Bandpass filtering is a convolution. Your project checklist requires you to demonstrate a property of it. So it is worth ten minutes of genuine understanding rather than treating np.convolve as a black box.

The recipe has four steps, and only the first is surprising:

  1. Flip the kernel back to front.
  2. Slide it to position n.
  3. Multiply each overlapping pair of values.
  4. Sum those products. That single number is output sample n.

Then slide one step and repeat. Step through it below.

Plain English

Kernel is the small pattern you slide along. It is also called the impulse response, because it is literally what the filter outputs if you feed it a single spike. Those two words mean the same object, and papers switch between them without warning.

Why flip at all?

The flip is what makes convolution behave properly when you chain filters together: running filter A then filter B gives the same result as running B then A, and that is only true with the flip in place. Every DSP library does it for you. For symmetric kernels, which most of yours will be, flipping changes nothing at all — but knowing it is there stops the textbook formula from looking arbitrary.

04 — The theorem

Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency

This is the result your checklist asks you to demonstrate, and it is the single most useful identity in the whole course.

conv(x, h) ≡ IFFT( FFT(x) × FFT(h) )

The left side is the slow flip-slide-multiply-sum from Figure 3. The right side transforms both signals, multiplies them point by point, and transforms back. They give identical numbers, not merely similar ones.

Two reasons to care. First, it is fast: convolution costs roughly N² operations while the FFT route costs about N log N, which for long signals is an enormous difference. Second, and more usefully for understanding, it means filtering is just reshaping a spectrum. A bandpass filter is a spectrum that is 1 inside your band and 0 outside; multiplying by it deletes everything outside the band. That is a far more intuitive picture of filtering than sliding a kernel.

Whenever you filter through the FFT, zero-pad first. Or use a library function like scipy.signal.fftconvolve, which does it for you.
Part C

Sampling and the DFT

05 — The camera's limit

Sampling, Nyquist, and aliasing

Your webcam does not record the world continuously. It takes a snapshot 30 times a second and throws away everything in between. The sampling theorem tells you what survives that:

To capture a rhythm at frequency f, you must sample at more than 2f. Half your sample rate is the Nyquist frequency, and it is a hard ceiling.

At 30 fps your Nyquist is 15 Hz, which is 900 BPM. Your search band tops out at 4 Hz, or 240 BPM. You have a factor of nearly four in hand, so for heart rate specifically, 30 fps is comfortably sufficient. This is not a constraint you need to worry about.

What happens above the ceiling is worth seeing, though, because it explains a class of bug that looks impossible. Drag the sample rate down.

The version of this that will actually bite you

Webcams drop frames. A camera nominally at 30 fps delivers 29, then 31, then stalls for 80 ms when something else grabs the CPU. Every figure in Tier 0 assumed samples are evenly spaced in time, and dropped frames break that assumption silently: no error, no warning, just a slightly wrong answer.

The fix is not complicated, and you should build it in from day one. Record the actual timestamp of every frame rather than assuming n/30, then resample onto an even grid before the FFT with np.interp. Two extra lines, and it removes an entire category of unexplainable error.

06 — Reading a real spectrum

Leakage, windowing, and the zero-padding illusion

In Tier 0 the mismatched frequencies cancelled perfectly and gave exactly zero. That was rigged: I used a whole number of cycles. Real recordings never do.

When the signal does not fit a whole number of times into your window, the cancellation is incomplete, and the leftovers spray across the whole spectrum. That is spectral leakage, and it is why a real spectrum has a lumpy floor rather than a flat one. Leakage can bury a weak second signal under the skirts of a strong one.

The cause is the abrupt cut at each end of your window. The signal is happily oscillating and then simply stops, and that sudden edge is itself a sharp feature that needs a broad spread of frequencies to describe. The fix is to fade the window in and out gently instead, which is what a window function like Hann does.

Zero-padding adds points, not information. It interpolates between the bins you already had. It genuinely helps you locate the top of a peak more precisely, and it never helps you separate two peaks that have merged.

The honest summary of the three knobs

KnobWhat it genuinely fixesWhat it costs
Longer windowReal resolution. The only thing that separates close peaks.Slower response; heart rate changes get smeared together.

07 — Reference

Tier 1 vocabulary, plainly

Fundamental
The actual repeat rate of a signal. For you, the heart rate itself.
Harmonic
An echo at a whole multiple of the fundamental. The price of the waveform being sharp rather than smooth.
Fourier series
The claim that any repeating shape is a stack of sine waves at multiples of one base rate.
Convolution
Flip a kernel, slide it along, multiply the overlaps, sum them. Every filter is this.
Kernel
The little pattern you slide. Same thing as impulse response.
Impulse response
What a filter outputs when fed a single spike. Same thing as the kernel.
LTI system
Linear and time-invariant: doubling the input doubles the output, and the filter behaves the same today as tomorrow. Every filter you will write is one, which is exactly why Fourier analysis applies to it.
Convolution theorem
Convolving in time equals multiplying spectra in frequency. Makes filtering fast, and makes it intuitive.
Zero-padding
Appending zeros before an FFT. Required before FFT-based filtering to prevent wraparound. Also interpolates the spectrum, but adds no real resolution.
Circular convolution
What the FFT does if you forget to pad: it treats the signal as a loop, so the end contaminates the beginning.
Sampling rate (fs)
Snapshots per second. Your webcam's frame rate.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sampling rate. The hard ceiling on what can be captured. At 30 fps it is 15 Hz.
Aliasing
A rhythm above Nyquist masquerading convincingly as a slower one. Undetectable after the fact.
Spectral leakage
Energy smeared across the spectrum because the window cut the signal mid-cycle.
Window function
A gentle fade applied to each end of your data before the FFT. Hann is the sensible default.
DFT / FFT
The winding machine run at every bin frequency. FFT is the fast algorithm; DFT is the definition.
08 — Before Tier 2

Check yourself

Your system reports 150 BPM for a subject who is visibly calm. What is your first hypothesis, and how do you test it in one line?

You have locked onto the second harmonic, and the true rate is 75 BPM.

Test it by looking at half your reported frequency. If there is a real peak at 75 BPM that is merely shorter than the one at 150, you have your answer. A calm adult at 150 is far less likely than a sharp pulse waveform with a suppressed fundamental.

Worth checking too: an over-aggressive high-pass filter attenuating 1.25 Hz while leaving 2.5 Hz untouched is a very common cause of the suppressed fundamental in the first place.

You filter through the FFT and every window has a strange spike in its first few samples. What did you forget?

Zero-padding. The FFT treats your signal as looping, so the tail of the window wrapped around and contaminated the head. That is circular convolution rather than the linear convolution you wanted.

Pad both signals to at least len(x) + len(h) - 1 before transforming, or just call scipy.signal.fftconvolve, which handles it.

Two peaks in your spectrum have merged into one hump. Does zero-padding fix it?

No. Never. Zero-padding draws the same curve with more points, so the hump becomes smoother and prettier while remaining a single hump.

Only a longer recording adds real resolution, because only more time lets two close rhythms drift far enough apart to be distinguished. If you cannot record longer, you cannot separate them, and the honest move is to report lower confidence rather than a precise-looking wrong number.

Your webcam claims 30 fps but drops frames under load. Which figure on this page stops being true?

All of them, mildly, and Tier 0's winding machine most fundamentally. Every one assumes samples are evenly spaced in time. Winding a signal assigns each sample an angle based on when it happened, so if the true timing differs from the assumed n/30, every dot is placed at slightly the wrong angle and the pile blurs.

The effect is a smeared, shorter peak and a slightly wrong BPM, with no error message anywhere. Record real timestamps and resample onto an even grid with np.interp before the FFT.
